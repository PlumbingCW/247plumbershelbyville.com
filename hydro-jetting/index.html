Jet or cable? Choosing the right tool

A cable cuts a hole through a clog. A jetter washes the pipe wall clean. That difference decides which tool a plumber should bring, and it is why hydro jetting is the answer when a drain keeps clogging after every cabling.

Cable versus hydro jetter on a main line
QuestionDrum cableHydro jetter
What it doesBores a channel through the plugScours the full wall and flushes debris out
Best forOne-time clog, paper, a soft blockageGrease, scale, packed roots, repeat clogs
Leaves behindA coating of grease or scale on the wallA clean pipe interior when done right
RiskCable can tear on a broken jointWrong pressure can stress a weak old pipe
Needs a camera first?HelpfulYes, always

We run a sewer camera before the jetter goes in. Pushing several thousand PSI of water into a line you have not seen is how a worn clay pipe turns into a collapsed one. If the camera shows a broken section, the plan changes to repair and not scouring.

When a backup needs a jetter right now

Most calls start with a cable and end there. We bring the jetter when the cable comes back with clues: a rope of grease, a tangle of fine hair-like roots, or flakes of orange-brown scale that sound like rust. Those mean the wall is coated, and a hole through the middle will close up again in weeks.

If water is already on the floor, say so on the call. Dispatch will send a plumber with both tools on the truck, and you will not wait for a second trip. The 24/7 line takes these calls at night, on weekends and on holidays.

Grease buildup

Kitchen lines and any commercial kitchen drain carry fat that sets like candle wax. Jetting melts and flushes it, which a cable cannot.

Tree and shrub roots

Fine roots enter at joints and grow into a mat. A jetter with a rotating nozzle cuts and flushes them, and a cutter blade handles thicker ones.

Scale in cast iron

Old iron pipe grows rough nodules inside. These hold paper and slow flow until the line is nearly closed.

Sand, silt and debris

Floor drains and storm-connected lines collect grit. Water at force carries it out where a cable only pushes it along.

Repeat clogs

Three clogs in a year at the same drain is a pattern. Clean the whole wall and watch whether the pattern stops.

Before the jetter arrives

Steps that make the job faster and keep the damage small.

1

Stop all water use

Do not flush or run the washer. A jetter clears the line, but wastewater already in the pipe has to go somewhere first.

2

Clear the work area

Jetting needs access to a cleanout or a pulled toilet. Move stored items away from the basement wall or the yard spot near the house. Stay clear of the cleanout while the hose runs, because pressurized water and wastewater can spray back.

3

Note the pattern

Which fixtures are slow, how long it has been, and whether a cable was used before. The plumber reads the pattern like a map.

4

Keep people away from sewage

Wastewater on the floor carries bacteria. Keep kids and pets out, and do not touch it bare-handed. Open a window if the smell is strong.

What the visit looks like

1

Camera look

The plumber feeds a sewer camera down the cleanout to see the clog, the pipe material and any cracks, offsets or belly.

2

Pressure set to the pipe

Cast iron, clay and PVC tolerate different pressure. The plumber sets the pump and chooses a nozzle: forward-firing to open the plug, rear-firing to scour and pull the hose along.

3

Open the clog and scour the wall

The nozzle works up and down the run, usually twice. Debris is flushed to the sewer, not left in your line.

4

Confirm with a second camera pass

We look again to check the wall is clean, then show you the footage if the pipe has a defect.

Footage is easier to compare when it uses common terms. NASSCO's Pipeline Assessment Certification Program (PACP) is a defect-coding system that many sewer utilities use for cracks, offset joints and roots. Asking the plumber to describe findings in those terms makes a second opinion easier to get.

Where jetting is the wrong call

Jetting helps

  • Grease and soap coating in kitchen and laundry lines
  • Root mats at sound joints
  • Scale on cast iron that is still structurally sound
  • Repeat clogs with no pipe damage

Jetting will not fix

  • A collapsed or crushed pipe
  • A line with a large offset joint
  • A belly holding standing water
  • Pipe so thin that pressure could crack it

If the camera finds a broken pipe, jetting only hides it for a while. In that case ask about sewer line cleaning on a schedule, or a repair plan, and get the footage in writing.

Downtown buildings and restaurant drains

Buildings in the Shelbyville Commercial Historic District date from about 1822 to the 1930s, so a downtown drain can be a patchwork of cast iron, galvanized steel and newer plastic. Any commercial kitchen on that line adds fat every night.

That mix is the reason we camera first and jet second in older commercial buildings: it protects the weakest section. Grease interceptors and discharge limits are usually set by local sewer use rules, so ask the sewer utility before changing a kitchen drain. If you run a kitchen, ask about a recurring jetting schedule and see commercial plumbing for grease trap and after-hours support.

What changes the price of hydro jetting

The price depends on how much pipe needs scouring and how hard it is to reach. You get a number before the plumber starts.

Length and diameter of the lineA 25 foot branch is a short visit. A 4 inch main that runs 80 feet to the street takes longer and uses more water.
What is in the pipeSoft grease clears quickly. Dense roots or hardened scale mean more passes.
Access pointA cleanout at the house is simple. Pulling a toilet or working from a roof vent adds time.
Camera passesA before and after video adds labor but protects the pipe and documents the result.
After-hours callNight and weekend calls carry an after-hours charge. Same-day appointments are offered when available.
