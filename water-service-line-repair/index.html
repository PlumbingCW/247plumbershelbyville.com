Where the service line starts and stops

The service line runs underground from the connection at the water main to your meter, and then on to the shutoff valve inside your house. In general, the utility owns and maintains the part up to the meter, and the property owner is responsible from the meter to the house. Ask your water utility exactly where that boundary sits for your address before you hire anyone to dig.

That boundary matters because a leak on the utility side is a report to them, while a leak on your side is a plumber's job. A plumber who knows the pipe can tell you which side the loss is on and why. Replacement service pipe has to be approved for drinking water under the Indiana Plumbing Code, and the standard to look for on the pipe is NSF/ANSI 61 certification.

Soggy patch in dry weather

A yard strip that stays green and wet along the line from the street to the house.

Pressure fell off

Showers weak everywhere in the house, not just one faucet, points to the main line or valve.

Meter turning, nothing on

The dial moves with all fixtures off and the house main closed.

Water bubbling at the curb

Water rising near the meter box or sidewalk suggests a break close to the street.

Dirty or sputtering water

Air and sediment can come through a cracked or partly collapsed line.

Why service lines fail around Shelbyville

Frost is the local enemy. The City of Shelbyville lists a frost line depth of 30 inches, so a service line buried shallower than that is exposed to freezing and heaving. Lines that enter the house through a cold crawlspace or an unheated garage wall freeze in the same way.

Age and material matter too. Older homes may have galvanized steel, which corrodes from the inside and chokes the flow. Others have copper or plastic that cracked from soil movement, tree roots, or a past dig that nicked the pipe. Close to the Big Blue and Little Blue Rivers the ground stays wet longer, and shifting wet soil puts extra stress on buried joints.

Pipe material rules matter when a new line goes in. Polyethylene water service tubing is made to AWWA C901, and anything touching drinking water should be certified to NSF/ANSI 61. Federal law has banned lead pipe and solder in drinking water plumbing since the 1986 Safe Drinking Water Act amendments, so an older line deserves a material check. Ask your water utility what it has on record for your address.

How a plumber confirms the line is the problem

1

Meter check

Close the house main and watch the meter. Movement means the loss is between the meter and the house.

2

Pressure gauge

A gauge on an outdoor spigot shows static and flowing pressure, which separates a restriction from a leak.

3

Listening

Acoustic gear on the valve and a ground microphone along the route find the loudest point.

4

Locate and mark

Utilities are marked through Indiana 811 before any digging starts, and the pipe is traced to its entry point.

Low water pressure: line or something else?

Low pressure at one tap is usually a clogged aerator or a bad cartridge. Low pressure at every tap deserves a closer look at the line, the main shutoff and any pressure-reducing valve near the meter. A valve that is only partly open, or a regulator that is failing, mimics a cracked line.

Check if hot water is weaker than cold. If only the hot side is slow, the water heater's inlet or sediment buildup is more likely than the buried pipe. Test your tap water if you suspect mineral scale, and ask your water utility for its annual water quality report.

Dig, bore, or line it: repair choices

Open trench repair

  • Straightforward for short runs and single breaks
  • The failed section is cut out and replaced
  • Needs a lawn, driveway or walk to be cut and restored
  • Best when the pipe is shallow and the route is clear

Trenchless replacement

  • Pulls a new pipe along the old path or bores a new one
  • Disturbs far less yard, patio and landscaping
  • Needs entry and exit pits, and suits long runs
  • Not every soil or old pipe allows it

Dial 811 before any digging

Indiana 811 asks for notice at least two business days before you dig, and the service runs 24/7. Call 811, use 1-800-382-5544, or go to indiana811.org. Gas, electric and communication lines can lie in the same corridor as your water line, and hitting one is dangerous and costly. If you smell gas at any time, leave the area and call 911 and your gas utility.

When a broken service line is an emergency

A water line that has no water at all, a geyser in the yard, or water entering the basement is an emergency. Night calls are normal for us, since frozen lines often fail in the coldest hours. After-hours dispatch can arrange a plumber to make the line safe and turn water back on for the night when conditions allow.

Weekend service works the same way, and the 24/7 number is the one to use. For burst pipes inside the house, see burst pipe repair. Calls from Morristown and the rest of Shelby County go to the same line.

While you wait for the plumber

Steps that limit water loss and keep you safe.

1

Close the house main

Turn the shutoff inside your home clockwise. If water still flows, the break is before that valve and the meter shutoff may be the next stop.

2

Keep people off the wet area

A saturated yard can hide a washed-out hole. Keep kids and pets away from the spot until it is marked.

3

Do not dig

Digging without an 811 locate risks hitting gas or electric. Wait for the marks.

4

Store water if you can

Fill a few pots and a tub before the pressure drops further, so you have water for drinking and flushing.
