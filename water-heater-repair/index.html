Leaking tank or no hot water: which one are you dealing with?

Water heater repair means finding out why a tank stopped heating or started leaking, then replacing the part that failed. In Shelbyville, Indiana the usual culprits are a dead heating element, a worn thermostat, a burned-out thermocouple, a rusted drain valve or a corroded tank.

The two complaints look alike from the shower but they are very different jobs. No hot water is often a cheap part. A tank that is leaking from its own shell usually is not repairable, and we will tell you so before any work starts.

Water pooling around the base

Check the fittings on top first. If the water is dripping from the bottom of the jacket, the steel tank has likely rusted through.

Warm water that runs out in minutes

On an electric heater a failed lower element is the classic cause. On a gas unit, look at sediment packed around the burner chamber.

Pilot will not stay lit

A weak thermocouple or a draft near the combustion air inlet. A gas smell means leave the house and call 911 first.

Breaker trips when the heater runs

A shorted element or a loose wire at the thermostat. Leave the breaker off until a plumber checks it.

Rumbling or popping from the tank

Mineral scale at the bottom of the tank trapping steam bubbles. It means the heater is working harder than it should.

Rust-colored or smelly hot water

A spent anode rod is the usual cause. Replacing it can add years to a tank that is otherwise sound.

Before we arrive

Four steps that limit water damage and keep you safe.

1

Cut the power or gas

Flip the dedicated breaker off for an electric heater. On gas, turn the control knob to OFF. If you smell gas, leave the house and call 911 and your gas utility, then call us.

2

Close the cold supply valve

The valve sits on the pipe feeding the top of the tank. Turn the handle until it stops. That ends the flow into a leaking tank.

3

Do not drain the tank unless a hose is attached

Water inside can be hot enough to scald. If the tank is leaking badly, connect a garden hose to the drain valve, run it to a floor drain or outside, and open the valve slowly.

4

Move what the water can reach

Basement heaters sit on concrete, but boxes and furnace parts sit nearby. Lift cardboard off the floor and keep a mop or wet vac ready.

How a plumber works out what failed

We start with the water, not the tank. A leak that looks like the heater is often a weeping fitting, a dripping relief valve or condensation on a cold pipe, and none of those need a new tank.

Then comes testing. A multimeter checks each element and thermostat on an electric unit. On gas, we check the thermocouple output, the gas valve and the flue draft with a mirror or smoke pencil. Sediment shows up as a gritty flush from the drain valve.

1

Look for the water's true source

Dry every fitting with a rag and watch where the first drop forms. Top fittings, the T&P valve and the drain valve are checked before the shell.

2

Test the electrical or gas side

Element resistance, thermostat continuity and breaker amperage on electric. Pilot flame, thermocouple millivolts and burner condition on gas.

3

Flush a sample from the drain valve

Rust flakes, white grit or black sludge each tell a different story about tank age and water quality.

4

Give you the options and the price

You hear what is broken, what it costs to fix and whether a replacement makes more sense before anything is opened up.

Replacement parts should match the label on your tank. A temperature and pressure relief valve is made to ANSI Z21.22 and must never be capped or plugged, because it is the safety device that opens when pressure climbs. Gas storage heaters are certified to ANSI Z21.10.1 and electric tanks are listed to UL 174, so ask for parts that suit that listing.

On gas models the flue and gas piping work is guided by NFPA 54, the National Fuel Gas Code. That is why a plumber checks draft and leak tests every joint before leaving. After any thermostat work, the U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission advises a setting of no more than 120 degrees Fahrenheit to reduce scald risk.

Gas, electric and hybrid heaters fail in different ways

The fuel decides which parts wear out first. Most Shelby County homes run a standard 40 or 50 gallon tank, though hybrid heat pump models are showing up in newer remodels.

Hybrid heat pump heaters need breathing room

A hybrid unit pulls heat from the air around it, so a closet with no airflow makes it run on its resistance elements and the electric bill climbs. If your hybrid is cooling the room it sits in and still delivering lukewarm water, call us. A ducting or filter problem is often cheaper to fix than a compressor.

What goes wrong with tanks in Shelbyville homes

Houses in the West Side Historic District were built between 1853 and 1939, and many have been remodeled with water heaters tucked into basements or utility closets that were never designed for them. Cramped spaces mean poor venting and tanks that are hard to inspect. If your tank is wedged behind a furnace or under stairs, tell dispatch so we bring the right tools.

Hard water is the other local worry. Mineral scale builds on the bottom of every tank over time and insulates the water from the burner or lower element. Test your tap with an inexpensive hardness kit, and ask Indiana American Water for its annual water quality report so you know what is arriving at your house.

What decides the cost of a water heater repair

A simple element swap and a tank that has rusted through are very different bills. These are the factors that move the number.

Which part failedAnode rods, thermocouples and relief valves are small parts. Gas control valves and hybrid compressors are large ones.
Age and condition of the tankPutting a new element in a tank that is badly scaled or rusted may only buy a few months. We will say so plainly.
Fuel type and tank sizeA 50 gallon gas unit with a flue to disconnect takes longer than a 30 gallon electric under the basement stairs.

Repair it or replace it: how to decide

Repair makes sense when

  • The tank itself is dry and sound
  • A single part failed, such as an element or thermocouple
  • The heater is under about 10 years old
  • No rust shows in the drain sample
  • The flue and venting still meet code

Replace instead when

  • Water leaks from the tank shell or seam
  • The unit is past 12 to 15 years old
  • Repeated repairs in one year
  • The tank is packed with scale or sediment
  • You are changing fuel type or going tankless

If the answer is replace, our water heater replacement page explains the process, and a planned swap is covered under water heater installation. Homeowners who want endless hot water can read about tankless options.

After-hours failures, and why they cluster in cold weather

Heaters fail most when they work hardest. Indiana winters pull incoming water temperature down, so a tank runs longer to deliver the same shower and an old element finally gives out on a January morning. We take calls overnight and on weekends and offer same-day appointments when available.
