Overflowing toilet: what to do in the first minute

Do not flush again. Lift the tank lid and push the flapper down into its seat to stop more water entering the bowl. Then turn the oval or round stop valve on the wall or floor behind the toilet clockwise until it stops.

Once the water is stopped, you can look at what caused it. One clogged toilet usually means a clog in that toilet's trap. If the tub gurgles or another drain backs up too, the clog is farther down in the house drain, and a plunger will not reach it.

Likely a toilet-only clog

  • Only this bowl rises or drains slowly
  • A flange plunger or closet auger usually clears it
  • Other drains run normally
  • Often follows wipes, paper wads or a toy

Likely a main drain clog

  • Tub, shower or floor drain gurgles or backs up
  • Multiple toilets act up together
  • Plunging makes no difference
  • Needs cable or jetting, see sewer line cleaning

Signs a toilet needs repair or replacement

Runs after the flush

A warped flapper or a tired fill valve lets water slip from the tank into the bowl all day.

Phantom flush

The toilet refills on its own every so often. The flapper leaks slowly.

Weak flush or clogs often

Clogged rim jets, a low tank level, or a partial clog in the trap.

Water around the base

A worn wax ring or a loose closet bolt. The leak may be soaking the floor beneath.

Rocking bowl

A loose seat on the floor flange. Left alone, the wax seal fails and leaks follow.

Cracked tank or bowl

A hairline crack that seeps cannot be mended. Replace the unit.

Running toilet: finding which part is wasting water

Add a few drops of food coloring to the tank and wait 15 minutes without flushing. If color shows in the bowl, the flapper leaks. If the water level rises to the overflow tube and keeps hissing, the fill valve or float is set too high or is failing.

The cure is often a pair of inexpensive parts. Some one-piece models with a broken flush lever or a sealed tank need a plumber, since matching parts is harder.

Leaks at the base and a failing wax ring

A toilet sits on a wax ring that seals the bowl to the drain flange. When the ring fails or the toilet rocks, sewage-smelling water seeps out at the base each flush. The damage hides under the floor, so a stained or soft floor next to the toilet is a warning.

Fixing it means pulling the toilet, replacing the ring, checking the flange for cracks, and resetting the bowl level. If the subfloor has gone soft, that needs repair before the toilet goes back. Old houses with cast iron closet bends sometimes need the flange re-secured as well.

Toilet installation and replacement

A toilet that needs frequent attention is a candidate for replacement. Modern 1.28 gallon per flush bowls clear waste better than many old 3.5 gallon models while using less water. The rough-in distance from the wall to the drain center, usually 12 inches, decides which models fit.

A plumber checks the flange height, the shutoff valve, and the supply line before setting the new bowl. If the shutoff is old or seized, it is replaced at the same visit. See faucet and valve repair for how we handle stuck valves.

Two labels help when you shop. EPA WaterSense marks toilets that use 1.28 gallons per flush or less and pass flush performance testing, while the federal limit for new toilets is 1.6 gallons. ASME A112.19.2 is the standard for vitreous china fixtures, and ASSE 1002 covers anti-siphon fill valves, which stop tank water from being drawn back into the supply pipe.

How we diagnose a problem toilet

1

Water and shutoff check

Confirm the stop valve works and the supply line is intact before touching the bowl.

2

Tank inspection

Look at the flapper, chain, fill valve, float height, and overflow tube.

3

Trap and drain test

Use a closet auger on the bowl and test other drains for signs of a main line clog.

4

Base and flange check

Rock test for movement, look for staining, and pull the toilet if a leak is suspected.

5

Pick the repair

Present the cost of a repair against a replacement so you decide with the numbers in front of you.

While you wait for the plumber

Four moves that protect the floor.

1

Stop the water

Turn the stop valve behind the toilet clockwise. If it will not turn, close the main.

2

Do not flush or keep plunging

More water means more spill. Wait for the level to fall.

3

Contain and ventilate

Lay towels along the base. Wear gloves, because overflow water can carry waste. Keep kids and pets out.

4

Check other drains

Run a sink in another bathroom for a moment. If it gurgles, tell dispatch it may be the main drain.

Older plumbing and toilet drains

Homes built before about 1940 in the West Side Historic District, which was developed from roughly 1853 to 1939, may have cast iron or clay drain lines under the bathroom. A toilet that clogs repeatedly there might point to a damaged drain, not the bowl. Ask for a camera look at the drain if clogs return, see sewer camera inspection.
