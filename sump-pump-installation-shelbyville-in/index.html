Sump Pump Installation in Shelbyville, IN: Homeowner Guide

A proper sump pump installation in Shelbyville, IN comes down to four parts: a sealed basin, the right pump, a check valve and a discharge line that carries water well away from the house.

This guide covers each part and the limits of a pump in river country. See our sump pump page for repairs.

The four parts of a correct install

A sump system collects groundwater in a pit and pumps it outside before it rises through the slab. The pit, or basin, sits below floor level, usually in the lowest corner. A pump with a float switch sits inside, and a discharge pipe carries water out.

A check valve on that pipe stops water from running back into the pit when the pump shuts off. Leave it out and the pump cycles constantly. That is the most common shortcut in poor installs.

Sump system parts and what each does
PartWhat it doesWhat to watch
Basin (pit)Holds incoming water below the slabNeeds a gravel bed, solid walls or perforated sides, and a tight lid
Pump (submersible or pedestal)Moves water up and outSubmersible runs cooler in the pit, pedestal is easier to reach
Float switchStarts the pump at a set water levelCan jam against the basin wall, so it needs clearance
Check valveKeeps discharge water from falling backInstall it on the vertical run, with a small weep hole below it
Discharge lineCarries water outsideSlope away from the house and keep it free of low spots

Install steps a plumber follows

1

Pick the pit location

The lowest point of the slab or where water appears first. A plumber marks the cut, checks for rebar or radiant lines, and plans the discharge path.

2

Cut the slab and dig the pit

A concrete saw cuts a circle. Digging goes to the basin's depth, and about 2 inches of washed gravel goes in the bottom.

3

Set the basin

The basin sits level with its rim at or below the floor. Concrete patches around it, and a lid goes on to keep radon and humidity out.

4

Install the pump, check valve and pipe

The pump sits on the gravel or a brick, away from the wall. PVC runs up to a check valve and then out through the rim joist or foundation.

5

Test and add an alarm

Pour water in to trip the float. A water alarm or a battery backup is added on many installs.

Primary pump or backup too?

Primary pump only

  • Lower upfront cost
  • Fails when power fails
  • Works well for ordinary seepage
  • Needs a GFCI outlet and a dedicated circuit

Primary plus battery or water-powered backup

  • Keeps pumping through an outage
  • Backup pump sits higher in the basin
  • Adds an alarm and a charger to maintain
  • Water-powered units need adequate water pressure

Pump size follows how much water arrives and how high it must lift. Most homes use a one-third horsepower submersible, and a plumber steps up when the lift is tall or the inflow is heavy. An oversized pump can short-cycle, which wears the float and the motor.

Test the system twice a year by pouring a bucket of water into the pit. The float should rise, the pump should start, and water should leave through the discharge pipe without chattering at the check valve.

Discharge line rules of thumb

Where the water goes matters as much as the pump.

  • Route water outside, away from the foundation, not into a floor drain
  • Never tie a sump discharge into the sanitary sewer
  • Keep the pipe pitched so it drains, not freezes full
  • Leave the outlet clear of leaves and ice
  • Ask the building department what your town allows, and call Indiana 811 at least two business days before trenching the discharge route
  • Use a GFCI outlet and keep the cord off the wet floor

What the Big Blue River floods mean for a sump pump

The National Weather Service recorded the Big Blue River at Shelbyville cresting at 19.98 feet on April 6, 2025, the second highest on record and the highest since 1913. In March 1913, floodwater reached the first alley east of the Public Square and left over a fifth of residents homeless. That history is why a backup matters here.

A sump pump handles groundwater under and around a house. It does not hold back a river. When the river rises, groundwater rises too, so a pump may run nonstop, and a power outage then matters most. Plan for a backup, and keep the city's flood page handy.
